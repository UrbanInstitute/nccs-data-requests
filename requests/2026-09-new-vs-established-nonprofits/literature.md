# Literature memo: is the sector shifting to new nonprofits instead of established institutions?

Compiled 2026-09-03 by web search with page-level verification. Verification key: **verified** = the page or PDF was fetched and the number read there; **partially verified** = citation confirmed but the specific number came from search snippets or secondary pages; **unverified** = source could not be opened. Treat partially verified and unverified items as leads, not citations.

## 1. Organizational age, resource shares, and concentration

**McKeever, B. S. and Pettijohn, S. L. (2014). The Nonprofit Sector in Brief 2014. Urban Institute.** https://www.urban.org/sites/default/files/publication/33711/413277-The-Nonprofit-Sector-in-Brief--.PDF
Using 2012 NCCS Core data: 66.4 percent of reporting public charities had under $500,000 in gross receipts and composed under 2 percent of expenditures; organizations with expenses of $10 million or more were 5.3 percent of public charities and 86.5 percent of expenditures. Hospitals were 2.4 percent of public charities and 50.6 percent of expenses; education 17.0 percent of expenses. Size-based, not age-based; the baseline concentration any age analysis must net out. **verified**

**NCCS (2019). The Nonprofit Sector in Brief 2019.** https://nccs.urban.org/nccs/resources/sector-brief-2019/
Snippets: hospitals 2.2 percent of public charities, 49.8 percent of revenues, 50.6 percent of expenses. Page fetch failed. **partially verified**

**Foster, W. and Fine, G. (2007). "How Nonprofits Get Really Big." Stanford Social Innovation Review.** https://ssir.org/articles/entry/how_nonprofits_get_really_big
Of more than 200,000 nonprofits opened since 1970, 144 reached $50 million in annual revenue by 2003; roughly 90 percent of those studied had a single dominant funding source. **verified**

**Bradach, J. L. and Kim, P. (2016, orig. 2012). "Why More Nonprofits Are Getting Bigger." Bridgespan.** https://www.bridgespan.org/insights/why-more-nonprofits-are-getting-bigger
Of more than 200,000 opened 1975 to 2008, 201 (excluding hospitals and universities) passed $50 million by about 2011, up 40 percent from 144. **verified**

**Kelley, A. et al. (2024). "Funding Strategies of Large US Nonprofits." Bridgespan.** https://www.bridgespan.org/insights/how-nonprofits-get-really-big-2024
297 organizations founded after 1990 reached $50 million or more within about 30 years; more than 90 percent relied on a single funding category. The count of young organizations reaching scale has grown (144, 201, 297) but the threshold is nominal dollars. **verified**

**Salamon, L. M. (2002). "The Resilient Sector." Nonprofit Quarterly (excerpt of the 2003 Brookings/Aspen book).** https://nonprofitquarterly.org/the-resilient-sector-the-state-of-nonprofit-america/
1977 to 1997: nonprofit revenues grew 144 percent real; fee income accounted for 47 percent of sector growth; government revenue grew 195 percent real; private giving fell from about 18 to 12 percent of income. Growth was driven by fees and government, which favor large institutions. **verified**

**Giving USA Foundation (2025). Giving USA 2025.** https://givingusa.org/giving-usa-2025-u-s-charitable-giving-grew-to-592-50-billion-in-2024-lifted-by-stock-market-gains/
Total giving $592.50 billion in 2024. No breakdown by recipient age. **verified**

**Sato, G. (2025). "The 'invisible majority': What we know about very small nonprofits." Candid.** https://candid.org/blogs/data-insights-very-small-nonprofits-make-up-majority-us-nonprofits/
Very small = 990-N filers, nearly 60 percent of US nonprofits. Among organizations exempt 2019 to 2023, 22 percent of very small nonprofits received a foundation grant vs 72 percent of others; very small nonprofits received 0.4 percent of funding 2019 to 2023. 26 percent of very small nonprofits received exemption in the last five years vs 8 percent of others. The closest published age-by-resource-share evidence found; it points against a shift to new organizations. **verified**

Not found: Nonprofit Almanac age-by-share tables; Independent Sector revenue-share tables; any Candid product tabulating grant dollars by grantee ruling year. **unverified**

## 2. Entry, exit, formation, and Form 1023-EZ

**Harrison, T. D. and Oxley, J. (2025). "Nonprofit Entry, Exit, and Implications for Sector Growth." Nonprofit Management and Leadership.** https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/nml.21650
Entry 4 to 5 percent and exit 1 to 2 percent per year; lack of exit, not excess entry, drives continued growth. **verified (abstract)**

**Mayer, D. J. (2024). "Connecting founding and dissolution." Public Administration Review.** https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/puar.13825
County panel 2010 to 2016: one SD more dissolutions predicts a 1.5 percent higher founding rate. **verified (abstract)**

**Searing, E. A. M. and Lecy, J. D. (2022). "Growing Up Nonprofit." NVSQ.** https://doi.org/10.1177/08997640211014280
Almost half of all nonprofits at any time are early-stage with under $100,000 in revenue; professional fundraising and government funds predict formalization. **verified (abstract)**

**Kim, M., Andersson, F. O., Faulk, L., Harrison, T., and Lecy, J. (2022). Diversity in Nonprofit Entrepreneurship. Independent Sector.** https://independentsector.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/08/Diversity-in-Nonprofit-Entrepreneurship-Research-Paper-Independent-Sector-August-17-2022.pdf
NCCS Core 2000/2010/2019: over a decade a new nonprofit is almost twice as likely to cease operations as to grow beyond grassroots scale; under-$100k organizations in 2010 were three times more likely to die than to grow to the next size category. **verified**

**Hager, M. A., Galaskiewicz, J., and Larson, J. A. (2004). "Structural embeddedness and the liability of newness among nonprofit organizations." Public Management Review 6(2).** https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/1471903042000189083
Younger organizations more likely to close; effect reduced by volunteers, private donations, and size. **partially verified**

**Lecy, J. D. and Van Slyke, D. M. (2013). "Nonprofit Sector Growth and Density." JPART 23(1).** https://academic.oup.com/jpart/article-abstract/23/1/189/970240
Government funding has a more favorable effect on nonprofit density than private donations. **verified**

**National Taxpayer Advocate (2017). Annual Report to Congress, Form 1023-EZ.** https://www.taxpayeradvocate.irs.gov/reports/2017-annual-report-to-congress/most-serious-problems/
Sampled erroneous approval rates of 37 percent (2015), 26 percent (2016), 42 percent (2017); the IRS approves virtually all 1023-EZ applications. **verified**

**National Taxpayer Advocate (2018, updated 2023). "A Problem of the IRS's Own Making: Automatic Revocations of 1023-EZ Exempt Organizations."** https://www.taxpayeradvocate.irs.gov/news/ntablog-a-problem-of-the-irss-own-making-automatic-revocations-of-1023-ez-exempt-organizations/
About a third of the 15,000 organizations approved via 1023-EZ in 2014 had been auto-revoked by May 2017. **verified**

**IRS Exempt Organizations (2016). Form 1023-EZ Update Report.** https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-tege/form_1023ez_update_report_final.pdf
July 2014 to June 2016: 87,954 Forms 1023-EZ, about 55 percent of all exemption applications; 94 percent approved. **verified**

**Wyland, M. (2018). "How Many Nonprofits Are There?" Nonprofit Quarterly.** https://nonprofitquarterly.org/how-many-nonprofits-1023ez/
501(c)(3) approvals rose from about 37,000 in 2013 to about 94,000 in 2014 and stayed near or above 80,000 through 2017; more than 760,000 organizations auto-revoked mid-2010 to end-2017. **verified**

**Harris, K. (2025). "Are There Too Many Nonprofits?" Nonprofit Quarterly.** https://nonprofitquarterly.org/are-there-too-many-nonprofits-or-too-few-bridges-between-them/
Opinion piece: sector growing about 5 percent a year since 2013 (secondary sourcing). Framing, not evidence. **verified as stated**

Not found: a specific Urban Institute report on 1023-EZ by Boris/Steuerle/Tuckman (only Blackwood and Roeger 2011 "Revoked" surfaced). **unverified**

## 3. Financial metrics for a cohort-by-age analysis

**Tuckman, H. P. and Chang, C. F. (1991). NVSQ 20(4).** https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/089976409102000407
Four vulnerability indicators: equity balance, revenue concentration (Herfindahl), administrative cost ratio, operating margin. **verified (abstract)**

**Hager, M. A. (2001). NVSQ 30(2).** https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/pdf/10.1177/0899764001302010
Tuckman-Chang measures show promise for predicting closure for some organization types. **verified (abstract)**

**Bowman, W. (2011). NML 22(1).** https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/nml.20039
Long-run capacity vs short-run resiliency; months of spending as the resiliency measure. **verified (abstract)**

**Calabrese, T. (2013). "Running on Empty." NML 23(3).** https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/nml.21064
Significant numbers of nonprofits hold no operating reserves; size is not an important predictor. **verified (abstract)**

**Prentice, C. R. (2016). "Why So Many Measures of Nonprofit Financial Performance?" NVSQ 45(4).** https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0899764015595722
Caution in using financial ratios; supports a small metric set. **verified (abstract)**

**Hung, C. and Hager, M. A. (2019). NVSQ meta-analysis.** https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0899764018807080
Small positive association between revenue diversification and financial health. **verified (abstract)**

**Nonprofit Finance Fund (2025). State of the Nonprofit Sector Survey.** https://nff.org/state-of-the-nonprofit-sector-survey/2025-state-of-the-survey-nonprofit-sector-survey/
52 percent have three months or less cash on hand; 36 percent ended 2024 in deficit. No age breakdown. **verified**

## 4. Funders shifting toward newer organizations

**Smith Arrillaga, E. et al. (2025). Breaking the Mold: MacKenzie Scott's Big Gifts. Center for Effective Philanthropy.** https://cep.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/02/CEP_Breaking_the_Mold_FNL.pdf
Recipients had median prior-year budget about $7 million (vs $1.8 million median foundation grantee), median staff 50, median grant $5 million. No tabulation by founding year; the size profile is established mid-to-large organizations. **verified**

**Wormington, S. (2026). "Diversifying revenue sources." Candid.** https://candid.org/blogs/diversifying-revenue-sources-where-nonprofits-find-funding/
Survey of 3,888 nonprofits: founded 2010+ vs before 1990 reported foundation grants 81 vs 94 percent, earned income 42 vs 67 percent, government funding 32 vs 66 percent. Newer organizations have narrower funding access. **verified**

**Mowrer, E. (2019). "With Age Comes Success (In Grantseeking)." GrantStation.** https://grantstation.com/gs-insights/With-Age-Comes-Success
93 percent of organizations over ten years old won a grant vs 75 percent of younger ones (self-selected practitioner survey). **verified**

Trust-based philanthropy: only advocacy and primer material found; no empirical study of grant dollars shifting to younger grantees. **unverified (none found)**

## Recommended metrics

1. Total expenses by age cohort as a share of all reporting-organization expenses, with and without hospitals and higher education; report aggregate share and median together.
2. Program service expenses alongside total expenses (e-file Part IX), so disbursing means mission spending.
3. Grants paid (Part IX lines 1 to 3) for the funder-side question, where Schedule I allows.
4. Revenue concentration (Herfindahl over major revenue sources) per Tuckman-Chang.
5. Months of operating reserves and operating margin per Bowman 2011 and Calabrese 2013.
6. Cohort survival and 990-N share: the share of each ruling-year cohort that ever files a 990 or 990-EZ.

## Bottom line from the literature

No verified source supports a resource shift toward new organizations. The evidence found points the other way: very small, disproportionately young organizations receive 0.4 percent of funding (Candid 2025); post-2010 founders report narrower funding access (Candid 2026); MacKenzie Scott's recipients have a $7 million median budget (CEP 2025). Entry counts did surge after Form 1023-EZ in 2014, with high attrition (Taxpayer Advocate 2018, Wyland 2018, Harrison and Oxley 2025).
